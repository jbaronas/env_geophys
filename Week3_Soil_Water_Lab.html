How to use Lab A

What the lab shows

A soil profile 2 m deep: a topsoil (0–30 cm) over a subsoil, with a water table below. The first rain falls at hour 6; further bursts come 24 hours apart. Three virtual soil moisture probes, at 10, 30 and 60 cm depth, record the volumetric water content θv. A rain gauge records the rain, and a pressure logger in a piezometer records the depth of the water table. One run covers 5 days (120 hours).

The controls (left)

  • Soil profile: the topsoil and subsoil (the number after each soil is its Ksat in mm/h), the starting depth of the water table, and how wet the soil is before the rain.
  • Weather: pick a rain event, or set the intensity, the duration of each burst and the number of bursts (24 h apart) yourself. Evapotranspiration takes water out of the root zone between showers.
  • Run / Pause starts and stops the clock. You can change the rain settings during a run; changing the soil, the water table or the starting wetness starts the run again.
  • Restart clears the graphs and runs the same settings again from hour 0.
  • Reset all settings puts every control back to its starting value: loam topsoil and subsoil, water table at 1.6 m, dry start, a wet spell of rain (8 mm/h for 4 h, three days running), evapotranspiration 3 mm/day.

The panels (right)

  • Top row: running totals since hour 0, in mm. Rain that falls faster than the soil can take it in runs off as Hortonian runoff (infiltration-excess). Rain that falls on ground that is already full runs off as saturation-excess runoff. Net recharge is the water that reached the water table; it is negative if the dry soil pulls water up from the water table.
  • Water in the profile: θv against depth, now (blue) and at the start (dashed). The dotted brown line is the porosity φ: where the blue line touches it, the soil is saturated.
  • What the field instruments log: rain and runoff (top), the three probes (middle) and the water table depth (bottom), against time.

About the model

Net recharge is the flow across the water table; a negative value means capillary rise from the water table into the soil. The model solves the 1D Richards equation with Brooks–Corey soil properties (Rawls et al. 1982 texture-class averages), a water table that drains towards a stream over about two days, and a simple root-zone evapotranspiration sink. It leaves out cracks and root channels (preferential flow). It is a teaching model and simplifies a lot, but the behaviour is right.

How to use Lab B

What the lab shows

A constant-head permeameter, the standard laboratory test for the saturated hydraulic conductivity, Ksat. A column 10 cm across (cross-sectional area A = 78.5 cm²) holds a sample of length L. An overflow keeps the water level above the sample constant. The outlet tube ends Δh lower, so Δh is the head difference that drives flow through the sample. Three piezometer tubes show the hydraulic head at the top, middle (or layer boundary) and bottom of the sample. The sketch is drawn to scale vertically, so you can see L and Δh change.

The controls (left)

  • Sample: a material, or Mystery A or B. Tick Two layers to put a second material under the first.
  • Sample length L and head difference Δh. These are locked while water is flowing.
  • Clock speed: speed up time for slow samples. The stopwatch counts model time.
  • Reset all settings puts every control back to its starting value: fine sand, one layer, L = 30 cm, Δh = 20 cm, real time. Your lab notebook is kept; use its Clear button to empty it.

Reading the sketch

  • Supply and overflow. Water runs in all the time. Whatever the sample cannot take spills out of the overflow, so the water level above the sample, and therefore Δh, stays constant. With a slow sample nearly all the supply goes down the overflow.
  • The sample is saturated (tinted blue): water fills all its pores, in both layers.
  • Piezometer tubes. The water in each tube rises to the hydraulic head at its port. With the tap closed nothing flows, so the head is the same everywhere and all three stand at the reservoir level. Open the tap and they fall to the heads of the flowing water, matching the head graph on the right.
  • Blue dots move at the seepage velocity v = q/φe. With two layers the flux q is the same in both, so the water moves fastest through the pores of the layer with the smaller effective porosity φe.

Making a measurement

  1. Start opens the tap, empties the measuring cylinder and starts the stopwatch. Water flows down through the sample and drips into the cylinder.
  2. Wait until you have a useful volume: 100–300 mL for sands. If the cylinder overflows (1000 mL), the run is lost.
  3. Stop and record closes the tap and writes the volume V and time t (whole seconds) into the lab notebook.
  4. Work out Ksat with the steps at the bottom of the page. The graph of Q against i collects all your runs for the current sample.

Calculating Ksat, step by step

Darcy's law for the column, rearranged for the saturated hydraulic conductivity:

Ksat = Q LA Δh = QA i
