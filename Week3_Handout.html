1 · Why it matters

  • Soil moisture is only about 0.05 % of Earth's fresh water (USGS), yet 78 % of the water used to grow the world's crops is rain stored in soil rather than irrigation (Mekonnen & Hoekstra 2011).
  • Groundwater supplies about a third of England's public water (BGS). Worldwide, levels are falling by more than 10 cm a year in 36 % of monitored aquifer systems (Jasechko et al. 2024). In County Durham, about 150 000 people drink water pumped from the Magnesian Limestone (Reker et al. 2019).
  • Damaged soils cost England and Wales about £1.2 bn a year (Graves et al. 2015): £566 m from lost soil carbon (organic matter in ploughed peat and clay soils breaking down to CO₂, priced at £51 per tonne of CO₂), £472 m from compaction and £177 m from erosion. About 80 % is paid off the farm, ultimately by consumers and tax-payers (CCC 2019).

So this week asks: how much water is in the ground, how tightly is it held, how fast does it move, and where does it go?

2 · Key ideas

2.1 Where water lives in the ground

Above the water table, pores hold both water and air: the unsaturated (vadose) zone. Just above the water table, capillary forces keep the pores nearly full: the capillary fringe. Below the water table every pore is full of water: the saturated zone, where groundwater flows.

Water table

The level below which every pore is full of water. Water in a well or borehole settles at this level.

2.3 Why soils hold water

Cohesion: water molecules attract each other through hydrogen bonds. Adhesion: they also attract solid surfaces, such as soil minerals. Surface tension: at an air–water surface, cohesion pulls the surface tight like a stretched skin (72.8 mN/m at 20 °C), which is why a pond skater can stand on water.

Together, adhesion and surface tension pull water into narrow pores and hold it there. In a clean tube of radius r, water rises h = 0.15 cm² / r (Weil & Brady Eq. 5.1): a pore with r = 0.001 cm lifts water 0.15 cm² / 0.001 cm = 150 cm.

Capillarity

Narrow pores pull water in and up, and hold it, by adhesion and surface tension. The narrower the pore, the stronger the pull.

Matric potential: how tightly soil holds water

Matric potential (ψm)

The pressure of water held in pores by adhesion and surface tension, in kPa. It is 0 when the soil is saturated and becomes more negative as the soil dries. Suction is the same number without the minus sign: −33 kPa of matric potential is 33 kPa of suction.

A tensiometer measures it: a water-filled tube with a porous ceramic tip, buried in the soil. Dry soil pulls water out through the tip until the water in the tube is at the same potential as the soil water; a vacuum gauge then reads ψm. Tensiometers work down to about −85 kPa, so they suit moist soils.

Soil water potential: the full picture

Soil water potential (ψ)

The energy of soil water compared with pure, free water at the same height and air pressure (kPa). Water moves from higher to lower potential.

ψ = ψg + ψm + ψo, plus ψh below the water table. Gravity (ψg) and the weight of water below the water table (ψh) are positive. Matric (ψm) and osmotic (ψo) potentials are negative: water held by surfaces, or surrounded by dissolved salts, has less energy than free water, so removing it takes a pull. Dividing a potential by the weight of water turns it into a height: 10 kPa ≈ 1 m of water (Weil & Brady Table 5.1).

Degrees of soil wetness

Field capacity (FC)

Water left once gravity drainage stops, 1–3 days after soaking: ψm ≈ −10 to −30 kPa.

Wilting coefficient

Water left when roots can take no more and plants wilt for good (permanent wilting point): ψm ≈ −1500 kPa.

Available water-holding capacity (AWHC)

Field capacity minus the wilting coefficient: the water plants can use.

For the Lab A soils, in the top 30 cm: sand 12 mm, loam 35 mm, silt loam 45 mm, clay 34 mm. Clay holds the most water, but much of it too tightly for roots. Each 1 % of organic matter adds about 4 mm of available water per 10 cm of a silt loam (Weil & Brady Fig. 5.38).

2.4 Evapotranspiration: how plants empty the soil

Evaporation (E): water turns to vapour from soil, water and wet leaves, driven by the Sun's energy. Transpiration (T): water drawn up through plants and lost as vapour through the stomata, small pores in the leaves. Plants must open their stomata to take in CO₂, so they lose water as they grow; when water is short they close them, and growth stops.

Evapotranspiration (ET)

Water returned to the air by evaporation and transpiration together, in mm per day, like rainfall.

Transpiration keeps the leaves at a low potential, so water is pulled up from the soil. As the soil dries its matric potential falls, and at about −1500 kPa most plants can no longer keep up: the wilting coefficient. Potential ET is under 1 mm a day in a temperate winter and up to 10–12 mm a day in hot, dry wind (Weil & Brady 6.3).

2.5 Infiltration and runoff

Dry soil pulls rain in by suction as well as gravity, so infiltration is fast at first and then settles towards the saturated hydraulic conductivity, Ksat.

Saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ksat)

How fast water flows through soil (or rock) when every pore is full and only gravity pulls: a speed, in mm/h or m/s.

Infiltration capacity

The fastest rate at which the soil surface can take in water (mm/h). Compare it with the rain intensity, in the same units.

RunoffWhen it happensTypical places
Infiltration-excess (Hortonian)Rain falls faster than the soil can take it inCompacted fields, tracks, bare or sealed ground, summer downpours
Saturation-excess (Dunne)The ground is already full; even gentle rain cannot get inValley floors near streams, wet uplands, after long wet spells

Land use matters: at Pontbren in mid-Wales, five years after native trees were planted on sheep pasture, infiltration was 67 times faster and surface runoff 78 % lower than in grazed pasture (Marshall et al. 2014).

2.6 Unsaturated flow and wetting fronts

Above the water table, water moves from wet soil (weak suction) towards dry soil (strong suction), and downwards under gravity. How fast depends on the unsaturated hydraulic conductivity, K(θv), which falls a million-fold as the soil dries, because the big pores empty first. After rain, a sharp wetting front moves down. In sandy soils it moves mostly down and fast; in clayey soils it spreads sideways as much as down, and more slowly (Weil & Brady Fig. 5.20).

Wetting front

The sharp boundary between wet soil above and drier soil below, moving down as water soaks in.

Preferential flow

Fast flow through cracks, root channels and worm burrows (macropores) that bypasses the soil matrix, so water and pollutants reach the water table much sooner.

2.7 Saturated flow: Darcy's law

Q = Ksat A ΔhL Q discharge (m³/s); Ksat saturated hydraulic conductivity (m/s); A area (m²); Δh head difference (m); L length (m)
q = QA = Ksat × i Darcy flux (m/s); hydraulic gradient i = Δh / L
v = qφe seepage velocity: the real speed of water in the pores; φe = effective porosity

Units check: m/s × m² × m/m = m³/s.

Darcy's law

Flow through a saturated material is proportional to its Ksat, its cross-sectional area and the hydraulic gradient.

Darcy flux (q)

Discharge per unit of the whole cross-section (m/s), as if water flowed through the grains too.

Seepage velocity (v)

The real average speed of water in the connected pores: q divided by the effective porosity φe, which is a little less than the total porosity φ.

Hydraulic head (h = z + ψ)

The height water rises to in a piezometer: elevation z plus pressure head ψ (m). Water flows from high to low head.

Darcy's law also works above the water table, with two changes. The pressure head ψ is negative: it is the matric potential written as a height (−10 kPa ≈ −1 m of water), so dry soil pulls water in as well as gravity. And the conductivity depends on how wet the soil is:

Unsaturated hydraulic conductivity (K(θv))

The conductivity of soil that is only partly wet. Lower than Ksat, because water can flow only through the smaller pores that are still full, and it falls steeply as the soil dries.

Layers: when water flows across layers (rain soaking down through till), the least permeable layer controls the flow, and water can pile up above it. When it flows along layers, the most permeable layer dominates.

Perched water table

Water held up by a layer with low Ksat (clay, dense till), above the regional water table.

2.8 Aquifers in the UK

Aquifer

Rock or sediment that holds groundwater and lets it flow fast enough to supply wells and springs.

  • Durham: glacial till covers much of the Magnesian Limestone aquifer. In the Garmondsway borehole the water table rises in winter and falls all summer, even in wet summers (the wet winter of 2020–21 raised it by 9 m).
  • Mine water: beneath the limestone lie the Coal Measures and their old workings. When the collieries closed in the early 1990s, pumping stopped and mine water rose; old shafts and faults connect the workings to the aquifer, and sulphate at one public supply borehole rose from 30–40 mg/L to over 600 mg/L. The Coal Authority now pumps and treats about 2 200 million litres a year at Dawdon to hold mine water below the aquifer (Reker et al. 2019).
  • London: pumping from the Chalk lowered the water level under central London to 88 m below sea level by the 1960s. When industry left, levels rose again and threatened the Underground and deep foundations, so extra pumping was licensed from 1992 (Environment Agency).

Groundwater recharge

Water that drains below the root zone and reaches the water table. In the UK it happens mostly from October to March, when rainfall exceeds evaporation (Hughes et al. 2021).

2.9 From hillslopes to rivers

Baseflow

River flow fed by groundwater: slow and steady, it keeps rivers flowing between storms.

3.2 Across scales

  • Cosmic-ray sensors (COSMOS-UK, about 40 UK sites, including Moor House) count neutrons above the ground: wet soil absorbs them. They average over about 12 hectares (a radius of up to about 200 m) and the top 0.1–0.8 m (Cooper et al. 2021).
  • Satellite radiometers (NASA SMAP) measure microwaves from the top ~5 cm of soil every 2–3 days, on a 9–36 km grid.
  • GRACE satellites measure monthly changes in gravity, which track the total water stored in soil, groundwater and snow over areas of about 150 000 km². They showed groundwater in north-west India falling by about 4 cm of water a year in 2002–08 (Rodell et al. 2009).
  • Geophysics (Weeks 5–8 with Dr Jenkins: ERT, EMI, GPR) gives lines, maps and 3D images, metres deep.

3.3 Loggers in practice

How a probe works: an electrical pulse travels along metal rods in the soil. Water slows it, because water's dielectric constant (81) is far higher than that of minerals (3–5) or air (1); so the travel time gives θv. TDR also senses salinity from how much the pulse fades (Weil & Brady 5.4).

In practice: push the probes into the side of a pit at several depths, refill the pit, and leave a battery logger recording for months.

Limits: each probe senses only the 3–4 cm around its rods; clay, salty or peaty soils need calibrating against cores; stones or air gaps next to the rods spoil the reading.

How it works: a pressure sensor below the water feels the weight of the water above it plus the air pressure: 1 kPa is about 10 cm of water. A second logger at the surface records air pressure, which is subtracted (Freeman et al. 2004).

In practice: hang it on a fixed cable in a borehole screened only in the layer you want, and check the level by hand with a dip tape at every visit.

Limits: sensors drift and cables stretch (the dip-tape checks catch this); it gives the level at one point, for the layer the borehole is open to.

3.4 What controls soil moisture, and why it matters

What controls it

  • Weather: rain in, evapotranspiration out
  • Texture and organic matter: how much the pores hold
  • Structure and compaction: how fast rain gets in
  • Plants: roots dry the soil
  • Landscape: hollows and valley floors stay wetter; a shallow water table wets the soil from below

Why it matters

  • Crops: the water roots can reach
  • Floods: wet soil takes in less rain, so more runs off
  • Groundwater: recharge starts only once the soil is wet
  • Geophysics: water content changes resistivity and radar speed (Weeks 5–8)

3.5 Pore water conductivity

The electrical conductivity (EC) of pore water depends on how much is dissolved (more ions, higher EC), which ions (they carry current differently) and temperature (about 2 % higher for each °C warmer, so readings are corrected to 25 °C; Hayashi 2004). To measure it, draw pore water out through a porous tip and use an EC meter, or, in the lab, extract the water from a saturated soil paste (ECe; Weil & Brady 10.4). Units: µS/cm or dS/m (1 dS/m = 1000 µS/cm). The conductivity of the whole soil, which EMI and ERT measure, also depends on water content, porosity and clay. Next week you measure EC yourselves.

Today's key concepts

  1. Water table: below it, every pore is full of water.
  2. Gravimetric water content (θg): mass of water per mass of dry soil.
  3. Volumetric water content (θv): volume of water per volume of soil.
  4. Saturation (Sw): fraction of the pores filled with water.
  5. Capillarity: narrow pores pull water in and hold it.
  6. Matric potential (ψm): the negative pressure of water held in pores.
  7. Soil water potential (ψ): energy of soil water; water moves from high to low.
  8. Field capacity (FC): water left once drainage stops.
  9. Wilting coefficient: water held too tightly for plants to take.
  10. AWHC: field capacity − wilting coefficient, the water plants can use.
  11. Evapotranspiration (ET): evaporation + transpiration.
  12. Saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ksat): how fast water flows through saturated soil or rock.
  13. Infiltration capacity: the fastest rate the surface takes in water; faster rain runs off.
  14. Wetting front: the sharp boundary between wet soil above and dry soil below.
  15. Preferential flow: fast flow through cracks, roots and burrows.
  16. Darcy's law: Q = Ksat A Δh/L.
  17. Darcy flux (q) and seepage velocity (v = q/φe): water moves faster than q.
  18. Hydraulic head (h = z + ψ): water flows from high to low head.
  19. Unsaturated hydraulic conductivity K(θv): less than Ksat; falls steeply as soil dries.
  20. Perched water table: water held up above a low-Ksat layer.
  21. Aquifer: rock or sediment that stores and yields groundwater.
  22. Groundwater recharge: mostly October to March, when soils are full.
  23. Baseflow: river flow fed by groundwater.

Porosity (φ)Fraction of a volume that is pore space. Effective porosity (φe): the connected part that water flows through.
Water content (θg, θv)Mass of water per mass of dry soil (θg), or volume of water per volume of soil (θv).
Saturation (Sw)Fraction of the pore space filled with water.
Matric potential (ψm)Negative pressure of water held in pores (kPa). Suction is the same number without the minus sign.
Field capacityWater left after gravity drainage stops, 1–3 days after soaking.
Wilting coefficientWater left when plants can take no more and wilt for good (also called the permanent wilting point).
AWHCAvailable water-holding capacity: field capacity minus the wilting coefficient.
Evapotranspiration (ET)Evaporation plus transpiration, in mm per day.
Infiltration capacityThe fastest rate at which a soil can take in water at its surface.
Hydraulic head (h)Elevation + pressure head; water flows from high to low head.
Ksat and K(θv)Saturated hydraulic conductivity (all pores full), and the lower conductivity of partly wet soil.
PermeabilityHow easily a material lets a fluid through; for water we measure it as Ksat.
Darcy flux (q)Flow per unit total area; not the true water speed.
Seepage velocity (v)Average speed of water in the pores, q/φe.
Perched water tableSaturated zone held up above a low-Ksat layer, above the regional water table.
RechargeWater that drains down to the water table.
Aquifer / aquitardA unit that yields useful groundwater / one that barely transmits it.
Throughflow / baseflowLateral flow through the soil / river flow fed by groundwater between storms.
