Part 1 · How much water is in the soil?

Water table: the level below which every pore is full of water. Above it is the unsaturated (vadose) zone, with the capillary fringe just above the water table.

Gravimetric water content (θg): mass of water per mass of dry soil.

Volumetric water content (θv): volume of water per volume of soil: what soil moisture probes measure.

Saturation (Sw): fraction of the pore space filled with water: 0 dry, 1 saturated.

Measuring: cores (weigh, dry at 105 °C, weigh again) are the reference. Probes and loggers record θv and the water level continuously at one point; cosmic-ray sensors, satellites and geophysics (Weeks 5–8) average over larger areas.

Part 2 · Infiltration and unsaturated flow

Cohesion, adhesion, surface tension: water molecules attract each other (cohesion) and solid surfaces (adhesion); at an air–water surface cohesion pulls the surface tight.

Capillarity: narrow pores pull water in and up and hold it: rise h = 0.15 cm² / r (h and pore radius r in cm).

Matric potential (ψm): the negative pressure of water held in pores: 0 kPa when saturated, more negative as soil dries. Suction is the same number without the minus sign. Measured with a tensiometer (to about −85 kPa).

Soil water potential (ψ): energy of soil water compared with pure, free water: ψ = ψg + ψm + ψo (+ ψh below the water table). Water moves from high to low potential.

Field capacity (FC): water left once gravity drainage stops, 1–3 days after soaking (ψm ≈ −10 to −30 kPa).

Wilting coefficient: water left when plants can take no more and wilt (ψm ≈ −1500 kPa).

Available water-holding capacity (AWHC): field capacity − wilting coefficient: the water plants can use.

Evapotranspiration (ET): evaporation + transpiration through plant stomata, in mm per day.

Saturated hydraulic conductivity (Ksat): how fast water flows through soil when every pore is full and only gravity pulls (mm/h or m/s).

Infiltration capacity: the fastest rate the soil surface can take in water. It is high in dry soil and falls towards Ksat as the soil wets.

Hortonian (infiltration-excess) runoff: rain falls faster than the infiltration capacity.

Saturation-excess runoff: rain falls on ground that is already saturated, however gently.

Wetting front: the sharp boundary between wet soil above and drier soil below, moving down as water soaks in.

Preferential flow: fast flow through cracks, root channels and burrows that bypasses the soil matrix.

Part 3 · Saturated flow: Darcy's law and groundwater

Q= Ksat A ΔhLDarcy's law
i= ΔhLhydraulic gradient (no units)
q= QA = Ksat × iDarcy flux
v= qφeseepage velocity: the real speed in the pores
t= distancevtravel time
h= z + ψhydraulic head

Darcy's law: flow through a saturated material is proportional to its Ksat, its cross-sectional area and the hydraulic gradient.

Darcy flux and seepage velocity: q spreads the flow over the whole area, grains included; water really moves faster, at v = q/φe.

Hydraulic head (h): the height water rises to in a piezometer; water flows from high to low head.

Unsaturated conductivity, K(θv): lower than Ksat, because water flows only through the pores that are still full; it falls steeply as soil dries. Above the water table ψ is negative.

Porosity is not permeability: what matters is how big the pores are and whether they connect. Clay has the most pore space but tiny pores.

Perched water table: water held up by a layer with low Ksat (clay, dense till), above the regional water table.

Aquifer: rock or sediment that holds groundwater and lets it flow fast enough to supply wells and springs.

Groundwater recharge: water that drains below the roots to the water table: in the UK mostly October to March, once soils are back at field capacity.

Task B2 · Is the flow rate proportional to the hydraulic gradient? (core)

Why measure at several heads? One run gives one value of Ksat, but it cannot tell you whether the test worked. Real permeameter tests are repeated at three or more head differences, and Q is plotted against i. If Darcy's law holds, the points fall on a straight line through the origin. The slope, fitted through all the points, gives a more reliable Ksat than any single run, because random errors in reading the volume and the time average out. A line that misses the origin points to a systematic error, such as a leak or a mis-measured head. A line that curves shows that the flow is not behaving as Darcy's law assumes. At large gradients, fast flow through coarse material becomes turbulent, or fine particles wash out of the sample and change it. At very small gradients the flow is too slow to measure well. Measuring at several heads shows the range over which the result can be trusted.

Part 4 · From hillslopes to rivers

Overland flow: fast flow over the surface: Hortonian (rain faster than the soil can take in) or saturation-excess (ground already full).

Throughflow: sideways flow through the soil, above a less permeable layer.

Groundwater flow and baseflow: slow flow through the aquifer to the river; the groundwater part of river flow, baseflow, keeps rivers flowing between storms.

A4 · Wetness before a storm
Hortonian runoff from the thunderstorm grows with the starting wetness: about 7 mm on a dry start, 10 mm on a normal start and 14 mm on a wet start. A dry soil pulls water in by suction, so its infiltration capacity starts higher and takes longer to fall towards Ksat. This is why the same storm causes bigger floods after a wet spell (antecedent conditions).
A5 · A sandy soil
Sand’s Ksat is about 210 mm/h, more than three times the lab’s maximum of 60 mm/h, and far above almost any real UK rainfall rate. So a natural sandy soil essentially never produces Hortonian runoff. It would take a surface whose infiltration capacity has been lowered, for example sealed, crusted or compacted ground (Task A6). Sandy areas do produce runoff where the ground fills up: saturation-excess runoff where the water table reaches the surface, or where water piles up in the sand above a much slower layer.
A6 · Compaction
Compaction cuts Ksat from 13 to 1.3 mm/h. In the thunderstorm (normal start), loam takes in about 30 mm and sheds about 10 mm as Hortonian runoff; compacted loam takes in only about 9 mm and sheds about 31 mm, three times as much. At Pontbren (lecture Part 2), infiltration under young trees was 67 times faster than in grazed pasture, and surface runoff was 78 % lower.

Lab B

B1 · Fine sand
At L = 30 cm and Δh = 20 cm, i = 20 cm / 30 cm = 0.67 and Q ≈ 0.52 mL/s (so 100 mL takes about 190 s of model time). Q = 0.52 × 10⁻⁶ m³/s; Ksat = 0.52 × 10⁻⁶ m³/s / (7.85 × 10⁻³ m² × 0.67) ≈ 1 × 10⁻⁴ m/s ≈ 8.6 m/day. Small differences come from reading the cylinder (±1 %) and the whole-second stopwatch.
B2 · Q against i
Yes: doubling Δh doubles Q, so the points fall on a straight line through the origin. That is Darcy’s law. The slope is Ksat × A (about 0.79 mL/s per unit gradient for fine sand); divide it by A to get Ksat. The slope uses all three runs, so it is a better estimate than any single run. In a real test you would also check that the line passes through the origin (no leaks or head errors) and does not curve (no turbulence, and the sample is not being washed out).
B3 · Mystery samples
(a) Mystery A: about 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m/s (about 1.3 mL/s at Δh = 20 cm). That lies in the ranges of clean sand, silty sand, peat and the low end of gravel; a clean medium sand is most likely. Mystery B: about 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s (10 mL takes about 44 days of model time). That is below every range except clay and glacial till. A value ten times away from everyone else’s is usually a unit slip (mL against m³, or cm against m). (b) The ranges overlap, so you need other properties. Texture: rub it between your fingers. Clay is smooth and sticky and can be rolled into a thin thread; till is a poorly sorted mix of clay, sand and stones, often with scratched, angular pebbles; sand feels gritty. Colour and organic matter: peat is dark brown and fibrous and light when dry. Grain size from a sieve or a particle-size test. Where the sample came from: the depth, the geological map and the landscape. Over most of lowland County Durham, a stiff clay with stones under the soil is the till left by the last ice sheet.
B4 · Two layers
(a) With the tap closed all three tubes stand at the reservoir level. Once the tap opens, the top tube stays there, the bottom tube falls to the outlet level, and the tube at the sand–clay boundary hardly moves: it stays almost at the reservoir level. So almost all the head is lost across the clay; the head graph is nearly flat in the sand and steep in the clay. (b) The two layers together behave like Ksat ≈ 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ m/s. That is close to the clay alone (10⁻¹⁰ m/s), a million times lower than the sand (10⁻⁴ m/s), and far below the average of the two (5 × 10⁻⁵ m/s). Flow across layers is controlled by the least permeable layer, so a till cover slows recharge to the aquifer below and protects it from pollution from the surface. (Notice that the blue dots move faster in the clay than in the sand. The flux q is the same in both layers, and the clay’s effective porosity is far smaller, so v = q/φe is larger there.)
B5 · Clay
Ksat = 10⁻¹⁰ m/s, so at i = 0.67, Q ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁷ mL/s: 10 mL takes about 2 × 10⁷ s, roughly 220 days. That is far too long for a laboratory test: the sample could dry, grow microbes or leak, and evaporation from the cylinder would be larger than the flow. Laboratories test clays with a falling-head test instead. A narrow standpipe sits on top of the sample, and there is no constant supply. As water seeps through the sample, the level in the standpipe falls, and you time how long it takes to fall from one height to another. Because the standpipe is narrow, even a tiny volume of flow makes the level drop by a measurable amount, so the test takes hours to days rather than months. Ksat comes from the standpipe and sample areas, the sample length and the ratio of the starting and final heights. Clays are also tested under pressure in sealed cells, which raises the gradient, and in the field with borehole tests, which also capture fractures that a small core misses (Task B7).
B6 · Seepage velocity
q = Ksat × i = 1 × 10⁻⁴ m/s × 0.01 = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m/s ≈ 0.086 m/day. v = q/φe = 0.086 / 0.32 ≈ 0.27 m/day. Travel time = 1000 m / 0.27 m/day ≈ 3700 days, about 10 years.
B7 · Core against borehole
(a) Any three. Fractures and joints in the till (from drying, freezing or ice movement), and root channels and worm burrows, carry water quickly; a small core usually misses them, while 5 m of borehole crosses many. Lenses and layers of sand and gravel inside the till do the same. A larger volume is more likely to include connected fast pathways (a scale effect). Drilling and trimming can squash the core and close its cracks. Ksat may also differ between horizontal and vertical directions. (b) The higher, field-scale value, and perhaps a worst case beyond it. Pollutants travel along the fastest connected pathways (preferential flow), so the core value would underestimate the risk about 1000-fold. Through fractures with a tiny effective porosity, water can cross 5 m of till in days to weeks rather than decades. This is one reason slurry spreading is restricted near boreholes and springs.
B8 · A landfill liner
(a) Take the base of the liner as the datum. The head at the top of the liner is 1 m + 0.3 m = 1.3 m, and at the base it is 0, so Δh = 1.3 m, L = 1 m and i = 1.3. q = Ksat × i = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m/s × 1.3 = 1.3 × 10⁻⁹ m/s; × 3.2 × 10⁷ s/yr ≈ 0.042 m/yr ≈ 42 mm per year over every square metre of the base. v = q/φe = 0.042/0.05 ≈ 0.83 m/yr, so leachate crosses the 1 m liner in about 1.2 years. A landfill produces leachate for decades, so a clay liner alone only delays leakage. (b) Darcy’s law: q is proportional to the gradient. With the leachate kept at a few centimetres instead of a metre or more, Δh and so q stay small; the membrane above the clay cuts the flow far further. (c) Any of: the clay dries and cracks before it is covered, or freezes; it was badly compacted or contains stones and roots; the leachate attacks the clay and changes its structure; tears or poor seams in the membrane; the drainage layer clogs, so leachate builds up and the gradient rises.
B9 · Soakaways on till
(a) No. Till has a Ksat of roughly 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻⁷ m/s, about 0.004 to 0.4 mm/h. A 100 m² roof in rain of 10 mm/h sheds 1 m³ an hour, while a soakaway with 10 m² of pit walls and floor in till could take in only about 0.004 m³ an hour at best. The pits fill and overflow. In winter the soil and till are already near saturation (Lab A, Task A3), so there is no storage left, and water ponds and runs off as saturation-excess runoff, flooding gardens and roads. (b) Sustainable drainage that stores water and releases it slowly to a stream: ponds and basins, swales, permeable paving over a storage layer, green roofs, rainwater harvesting. Digging through the till removes the low-permeability layer that protects the aquifer (Task B4). Runoff from roads carries oil, metals, road salt and nutrients, and it would reach the fractured limestone almost directly, with little time for filtering or breakdown, close to boreholes used for drinking water.
